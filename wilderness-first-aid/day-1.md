# Wilderness First Aid — Day 1 Notes

> **Disclaimer:** These are personal study notes from a Wilderness First Aid (WFA)
> course. They are a memory aid only — not a substitute for hands-on training. Always
> defer to your certified instructor and the official course materials, and follow your
> local protocols. Call 911 / activate EMS whenever it's available.

## 1. Scene Safety & Initial Assessment

Before touching anyone, size up the scene:

- **Is the scene safe?** Don't become a second patient. Look for hazards (traffic,
  rockfall, water, electricity, weather, unstable terrain).
- **How many patients are there?**
- **What is wrong?** What is the **mechanism of injury (MOI)** or **nature of illness (NOI)** —
  i.e., how were they injured, or how did they become ill?
- **Are there bystanders who can help?** Delegate tasks (call 911, fetch the AED, etc.).
- Put on gloves / PPE before contact.

---

## 2. CPR & AED

1. **Is the scene safe?**
2. **Check responsiveness** — tap and shout: *"Are you OK?"*
3. **Get help:**
   - Point to a specific person: *"You — call 911."*
   - Point to another: *"You — get the AED and come back."*
4. **Check breathing** — look, listen, and feel for ~10 seconds. Gasping (agonal
   breathing) does **not** count as normal breathing.
5. **If not breathing normally, start CPR:**
   - **Compressions:** rate **100–120 per minute**, push hard and fast, ~2 inches deep,
     allow full chest recoil.
   - **Ratio:** **30 compressions : 2 rescue breaths.**
   - If a **breath doesn't go in, reposition the airway** (re-tilt head / chin lift) and
     try again. Don't stop compressions for long.
6. **AED:** turn it on and follow the voice prompts; apply pads to bare, dry chest;
   stop and clear the patient when it analyzes/shocks; resume compressions immediately
   after.

## 3. Patient Assessment

### AVPU — level of responsiveness
Quickly gauge how awake/aware the patient is:

- **A — Alert**
- **V — responds to Verbal** stimulus
- **P — responds to Pain** only
- **U — Unresponsive**

For an alert patient, check **orientation** (often charted as **A&O × 4**):

- What's your **name**?
- **Where** are you?
- **What happened**?
- What **day** is it?

### SAMPLE — history
Gather the chief complaint and history:

- **S — Signs & Symptoms** (what you see / what they feel)
- **A — Allergies**
- **M — Medications**
- **P — Pertinent past medical history**
- **L — Last oral intake** (food or drink)
- **E — Events** leading up to the condition, injury, or illness

### LMNOPQRST — pain assessment
*(This is the LMNOPQRST variant taught in the course; the widely used core is OPQRST.)*

- **L — Location:** where is the injury or pain?
- **M — Motion:** is there any movement that makes it better or worse?
- **N — Night:** does it prevent sleep at night?
- **O — Onset:** when did it start? What were you doing?
- **P — Provocation/Palliation:** anything that makes it better or worse?
- **Q — Quality:** what does it feel like (sharp, dull, burning, cramping)?
- **R — Radiation:** does the pain travel anywhere?
- **S — Severity:** rate it 0–10.
- **T — Time:** how long has it lasted, and has it changed over time?

### DOTS — hands-on physical exam
Used when examining the body (especially someone who remains motionless). Look and feel for:

- **D — Deformities**
- **O — Open wounds**
- **T — Tenderness**
- **S — Swelling**

### SOAP — documentation
Quick way to document findings and treatment:

- **S — Subjective:** what the patient tells you.
- **O — Objective:** what you found / measured.
- **A — Assessment:** your best read of the problem.
- **P — Plan:** what you're going to do (treatment, monitoring, evacuation).

### Recovery position
For an unresponsive patient who **is** breathing normally (and with no suspected spinal
injury): roll them onto their side to keep the airway open and let fluids drain. Keep
monitoring breathing.

## 4. Shock

A life threat — treat early. **Signs:**

- Altered mental status
- High (rising) heart rate
- High (rising) respiratory rate
- Pale, cool, clammy skin
- Progressing to unconsciousness

**Treatment:**

- Lay the patient down.
- Elevate the feet/legs.
- Keep them warm (insulate from the ground too).
- Treat the underlying cause (e.g., stop bleeding).

---

## 5. Bleeding (hemorrhage control)

- **Find and stop life-threatening bleeding first** — this comes before almost everything else.
- **Always wear gloves.**
- **Expose** the wound so you can see the source.
- Have the patient **apply direct pressure** first if they're able.
- Apply a **pressure dressing**.
- **Wound packing:** pack large puncture wounds in **extremities only** — **do not pack
  torso wounds**.
- **Tourniquet:** apply **"high and tight"** above the wound for severe extremity bleeding.
- **Change/redress** roughly **every 12 hours** (or per protocol / if it soaks through).

### Amputations
- Treat the stump like any other serious bleed (pressure, dressing, tourniquet as needed).
- **Preserve the amputated part:** wrap it in gauze → seal in a ziplock bag → place that
  bag inside a **second ziplock with cool water or ice**. Keep it cool but **do not let it
  freeze or sit directly on ice**. Send it with the patient.

---

## 6. Impaled Object / Eye Injury

- **Do not remove** an impaled object — stabilize it in place.
- Build a **donut ring** from a triangular bandage and place it around the object to
  cushion and support it.
- For an **impaled object in the eye:** cover the injured eye, and **cover the
  uninjured eye too** with a **pinhole** in the covering — this gives the good eye a point
  of light to focus on so it stops moving (the eyes move together, so this rests the
  injured eye).

---

## 7. Burns

### Thermal burns
- Cover with a **dry, clean dressing**.
- Have the patient **sip water** (hydration).
- **Evacuate** if the burn is **2nd or 3rd degree**, crosses a **joint**, or is otherwise
  critical.
- **Automatically critical:** burns to the **face, hands, feet, or genitals**.
- **Any respiratory symptoms** (smoke inhalation, singed nose hairs, hoarse voice,
  coughing) = **critical** — airways can swell shut.

### Chemical burns
- **Dry chemical:** remove contaminated clothing and jewelry and **brush off** the dry
  chemical first, then **flush with copious water for ~15 minutes**.
- **Wet chemical:** same as above **but no brushing** — go straight to flushing.
- **Eyes:** irrigate **from the nose side outward** so you don't wash contaminant into the
  other eye.

### Electrical burns
- Treat the surface burn like any other **open wound**.
- Look for **both entrance AND exit wounds** (current travels through the body).
- **Check breathing and pulse** — electrical injury can stop the heart. **Start CPR if
  needed.**

---

## 8. Diabetic Emergencies

- Give **15 grams of fast-acting sugar** (glucose tablets, juice, etc.).
- **Wait 15 minutes.** If there's no improvement, **give another 15 grams** ("15/15 rule").
- Only give sugar to someone who can swallow safely.

---

## 9. Allergies & Anaphylaxis

- A severe allergic reaction can present with **full-body hives**, swelling, difficulty
  breathing, and shock.
- Treat anaphylaxis with an **epinephrine auto-injector (EpiPen)** if available and within
  your scope/protocol; then evacuate.
- *Course note:* the **Wilderness Medical Society** has a video demonstrating how to
  **re-use / "recharge" an epinephrine auto-injector** in a wilderness setting — review it.
